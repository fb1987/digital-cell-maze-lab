# Research audit: what the evidence supports after V11

October 18, 2026. This is a retrospective assessment, requested before further experiments. No candidates were executed, no new mazes were generated, and no evolution was run for this audit. Earlier source and raw results remain unchanged. New calculations use saved logs and maze geometry; they are reproducible with the accompanying [audit script](../scripts/research_audit_2026_10_18.py).

**I recommend continuing the research, while changing the immediate question from “what additional reward might help?” to “does this search process offer an achievable path from random rules to transferable behavior?”** We have evidence of evolved communication and some context-specific computation. We do not yet have evidence of a general random-origin maze solver, sustained improvement toward one, or a computational advantage over conventional solvers. The current approach has enough repeated negative results to warrant redesigning its learning conditions. Those results do not settle the much broader possibility of evolving local algorithms.

The earlier V11 proposal to add hidden-context discrimination should be deferred until the search and developmental timing are calibrated. This report is a recommendation, not a preregistration or an executed V12.

**There are three separate claims, with very different evidential support.**

| Claim | Assessment after V11 |
|---|---|
| A compact shared local rule system can implement maze solving. | Demonstrated in this project by the hand-written wavefront genome. Its core has 29 instructions; the richer runtime adds a stop instruction. |
| Evolution from random genomes can discover useful computation. | Supported for source-dependent signalling, goal-dependent intermediate responses, and a few specific fork decisions. |
| The current evolutionary method is discovering a general shortest-path solver. | Not demonstrated. Robust independent transfer is the missing step. |
| More heritable capacity, regulation, or evolutionary time could enable discovery. | Still plausible, but not established. V11 weakens the claim that raising the instruction ceiling alone is sufficient. |
| An evolved solver can beat strong conventional implementations on efficiency. | No supporting result yet. The successful seeded implementation is substantially slower than compiled BFS. |

Here, “general” should initially mean reliable transfer across fresh layouts, endpoint placements, orientations, sizes, trees, cyclic grids, and disconnected cases. A finite test suite cannot establish correctness on all possible mazes; that would ultimately need a structural argument or proof as well.

**The positive findings are real, but their scope matters.**

| Evidence | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| V3: source-dependent transmission on 174 fresh long-distance cases rises from 0.2480 frozen to 0.5090 under causal pressure, versus 0.4185 under the comparison pressure. Causal pressure leads in 2/3 replicates. | Heritable rules can improve a communication behavior that transfers to new mazes. Removing nonlocal reads sharply reduces that behavior. | A message can cross walls and flood a component without encoding a legal route. Long-route solving remains 0/174. |
| V5: a newly evolved local random-origin organism completes a fresh ten-edge path with three turns and two junctions. | More than adjacent-endpoint coincidence: a lineage acquired a useful response to spatial geometry. | Its hidden dynamics are identical under the alternate goal, and it fails all three 90-degree rotations. Goal-sensitive stopping is supplied by the common runtime. |
| V6: adding a goal-state pulse lets evolved rules improve the independent paired-output score from 0.0135 frozen to 0.0390; removing the pulse or nonlocal reads reduces the score to zero. | Evolution can recruit both inputs into intermediate output behavior. A stimulus convention materially affects discoverability. | The score is not route accuracy; complete paired routes remain 0/42. |
| V8/V8A: two variants make causal switched-goal fork choices on selected validation contexts; one requires two cooperating groups of edits. | There is a concrete example of mutation interaction producing a useful contextual computation. | Both variants make 0/12 strict paired choices and 0/24 complete routes on the fresh confirmation set. |
| V10/V10A: a new radius-2 register-routing edit supports a training fork decision and produces descendants. | Search can discover and reproduce a new useful local interaction within a program. | Child, parent, and root all fail every strict paired-choice test on 12 fresh matched layouts, at both near and far goal distances. |

Sources: [V3](source_pressure.md), [V5](factor_curriculum.md), [V6](goal_beacon.md), [V8A](junction_confirmation.md), and [V10A](distance_confirmation.md). The paired confirmation layouts are shared across compared models; their counts must not be multiplied into independent trials.

I would describe these as **precursors worth preserving**, rather than an emerging general solver. The distinction is empirical: when we move the goal, rotate the maze, or change the surrounding geometry, the stronger behaviors have not survived reliably. We have not yet shown a sequence in which one transferable competence becomes the foundation of another transferable competence.

**The negative results materially constrain our current strategy.** In [V1](first_evolution.md), random evolution matched its frozen control on four trivial corridor cases. In [V2](range_and_regulation.md), larger random populations solved only adjacent-endpoint cases and did no better than independent resampling. V4 and V5 did not establish an overall goal-conditioned transfer advantage. V7 improved a weak two-direction proxy, concentrated in one replicate, without complete paired routes. V9's stronger choice priority produced exactly the same matched population trajectories and RNG states as its comparison: qualifying organisms were already reproducing, so changing that ranking did not resolve transfer. V10's scheduled goal-distance curriculum also did not transfer.

V11 genuinely grew programs: evaluated genomes reached 228 genes, and the prepared next population contained a 240-gene genome. Yet the primary archives under both ceilings solved local 0/216 and multirange 1/216 fresh reachable tasks, matching frozen controls. Secondary local choice selections reached 4/216 versus 3/216 frozen under either ceiling. Every selected success was one edge. All selected models solved 0/180 distance-at-least-eight cases, 0/42 paired routes, and 0/18 strict paired choices. Conventional BFS, A*, and the wavefront baseline handled the reachable and disconnected tests. These are [V11 results](capacity_growth.md), not a new accuracy test.

That is strong evidence against a claim of useful general solving **under the tested setup and budgets**. It is weaker evidence about what a different representation, learning environment, or longer stable search could discover. The studies are also related continuations, not eleven independent replications from fresh random populations. Shared ancestry, retained elites, repeated archive labels, and three replicate families limit how much independent evidence we have.

**The experimental discipline has been a strength.** We separate reference paths from inference, test complete rasters, distinguish legality from optimality, use frozen controls, include cycles and disconnected grids, freeze models before fresh final layouts, record costs, and preserve populations and RNG states. Targeted source inspection found no oracle call inside the candidate inference loop. This audit is not a formal verification of every line. The saved intervention and ancestry analyses are unusually useful: they prevented us from mistaking a goal-independent growth pattern, a proxy score, or a validation-specific interaction for a general algorithm.

Those checks validate measurement and implementation. They do not establish that the search landscape is learnable. A large test suite can faithfully implement a fitness function whose useful intermediate solutions are extremely difficult to reach.

**The clearest new concern is developmental timing.** The later shaping objectives inspect a forced tick-16 trajectory. These probes suppress normal emission and quiescence stopping, while ordinary training inference permits up to 256 ticks and final evaluation up to 1,024, subject to earlier instruction/read limits.

The retrospective calculation finds that **all 211 successful frozen local wavefront outputs in V2 took exactly `2D + 1` ticks**, where `D` is the path's edge count. Of those, 160 had `D >= 8`, and all took more than 16 ticks. This strategy propagates information outward, then traces the selected route back. The later hard paired tasks require `D >= 8`; at tick 16 that developmental ordering has not yet connected its output back to the source. The junction/prefix reward requires a source-connected output, so it cannot yet recognize that progress.

This is the measured timing of a known strategy, **not a universal lower bound for every possible solver**. We did not rerun that exact seed under the newer goal-pulse convention. Completed correct solutions still outrank shaping in normal fitness. The concern is that incomplete ancestors of a sensible two-stage computation can receive poor intermediate credit while fast shallow growth receives credit. The main objective can recognize the destination while the intermediate objective makes the route to it difficult.

There is also a strict information limit on some local probes. On saved V11 regular pairs, both alternative goals lie more than 16 graph edges from the entire fork region on 10/252 training layouts, 1/12 validation layouts, and 2/18 final layouts. A deterministic nearest-neighbor program cannot distinguish those switched goals in that region by tick 16 under the forced probe. This is a necessary causal constraint, not a claim that all other pairs are solvable. Most pairs are within that bound, and nonlocal programs have different communication cones. Thus timing cannot explain the entire negative result.

The saved logs also show a mismatch between diagnostic and emitted behavior. Across V11's expanded multirange training populations, there are 670 single-goal strict-at-probe responses. In 371, ordinary inference had already ended before tick 16; 668 have invalid ordinary outputs. These are repeated response opportunities, not 670 distinct discoveries or successful two-goal pairs. “Ended” includes any terminal status, including budget limits. Forced probes can be useful measurements, but a state observed under continued development is not necessarily a state the deployed organism will emit. These counts do not show that a choice first arose after stopping; they show that the scored observation and ordinary termination occur at different times.

**Capacity and evolvability are different quantities.** The current organism is an eight-channel synchronous register program with gated instructions. Each site has 64 registers; scratch registers reset each tick. Longer genomes increase potential computation, but useful behavior requires operations to connect through the right registers, preserve memory, and express in the right conditions. Insertions, swaps, replacements, and random operand changes can disrupt these dependencies.

Duplicating a block copies its existing register references. It does not create an isolated new module with a separate interface. Likewise, doubling the gene ceiling does not add state channels or independently regulated functional compartments. This does not prove that modular operators would work better, and the small known solver shows that the language is already expressive enough. It does mean V11 tested extra program length under the same broad organization, rather than every form of the user's “critical mass of complexity and regulation” hypothesis.

About 49% of V11's coeval changed-DNA comparisons preserve the recorded behavioral fingerprint. This may provide neutral material for future discovery, but it is not evidence of growing useful complexity; hidden trajectories can still differ. Conversely, late populations retain 95–96 unique genotypes out of 96, so a claim that they have simply collapsed to identical DNA would be wrong. We need measures of causal functional diversity and heritable dependencies, not length or genotype count alone.

Preservation also consumes active search space. V11 permanently protects 18–36 organisms per 96-member population, or 18.75–37.5%. After those organisms, phenotype niches, and nine immigrants per generation, each population generates only **30.5–46.2 mutated children per generation**, about 1,465–2,218 over its 48-generation study. These birth counts include children saved in the prepared next population, which have not necessarily been evaluated. Protecting rare findings is valuable. Keeping every valuable historical genotype on disk does not require keeping all of them in every active generation. A bounded rotating archive is a future hypothesis to test, not a demonstrated fix.

**We have used meaningful compute, but have not given a stable search a large budget.** Summing the eleven saved main-run timers gives **208.43 minutes, or 3.47 hours**. This excludes separately recorded audits, diagnostics, tests, and development; it is not total project effort. V3 alone used 64.22 main minutes, including 60.45 search minutes. Most later main studies used 15–17 minutes across twelve conditions. Across V3–V11, saved search/validation records account for approximately **2.83 million ordinary responses and 5.67 million probes**.

These are substantial measurements, but repeated evaluations are not independent algorithm discoveries. Recent histories include scheduled continuations totaling 656 generations through V2–V11, under changing objectives and input conventions; individual ancestries differ because of immigration and elite reinsertion. It would be wrong to describe the project as only one 48-generation attempt. Equally, those 656 generations do not represent one stable, calibrated selection environment.

The answer to “are we too conservative?” is therefore **yes about uninterrupted evolutionary depth and effective mutant exploration; not obviously about substrate size or maximum gene count**. We have often used a short screen to ask whether an incremental change immediately transfers. That is useful for controlling costs, but a gate requiring early transfer can fail to test a hypothesis that many generations of precursor accumulation are necessary. A single precommitted 60–90-minute study is reasonable after calibration, even without an early exciting result. Its budget should remain fixed; it should not become an open-ended search until something succeeds.

**External work supports plausibility, not a promise about our implementation.** Earle and colleagues' [Pathfinding Neural Cellular Automata](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.06820v1) trains shared local rules for path output and studies generalization. Its method uses gradient training, oracle-derived raster targets, and 50,000 optimizer updates per trial; it is not discrete-genome evolution. It supports the representational idea and shows that training design matters. It does not establish that our mutation process will discover the rules or outperform BFS.

Lenski and colleagues' [digital-evolution study](https://cse.msu.edu/~ofria/pubs/2003LenskiEtAl.pdf) shows that rewarding simpler functions can support the evolution of complex coordinated functions. Its methods use a population capacity of 3,600 and a run duration equivalent to 15,873 ancestral generations, with an initially supplied self-replication mechanism. The numbers illustrate how small our per-condition runs are relative to one successful digital-evolution precedent; they are not a minimum requirement or a transferable compute estimate. Mitchell, Crutchfield, and Hraber also describe both innovation and impediments in [evolved cellular automata](https://csc.ucdavis.edu/~evca/Papers/MechImped.html), including early adaptations that obstruct later improvements. Path dependence is therefore a reasonable hypothesis to investigate, not an explanation already proven here.

**Efficiency remains a separate, harder objective.** On the successful size-17 samples, V1's seeded evolution took a median 318.25 microseconds versus 2.09 for compiled BFS; V2's corresponding seeded evolved-range arm took 1,002.41 versus 3.30 microseconds. These are comparisons within each study, roughly 152-fold and 304-fold slower. Different runtimes and samples mean they are not a longitudinal speed trend. Wrong random-origin outputs earn no speed credit. Our baselines are strong local implementations, not a survey proving that we tested the world's fastest available solvers.

The present runtime scans open sites, dispatches every gene, and repeats this for every developmental tick. Gates reduce executed operations but do not remove the scan and dispatch costs. Its dense worst-case work grows like `N × G × T`; its eight-channel double buffers alone use `32N` bytes, plus input, output, program, and other arrays. V11's 213.2 MiB process peak includes training/compiler/data overhead, so it must not be presented as the memory of one cell.

For bounded-degree unweighted grids, BFS has linear worst-case work; accepting a dense maze and emitting a dense raster already entails linear-size data. Any advantage must therefore come from constants, memory organization, useful parallel latency, or a specifically defined repeated-query/batched workload. Sparse updates, bit packing, specialization of a discovered rule, or parallel hardware could matter later. Adding geometric long-range reads alone does not certify a traversable connection across intervening walls. Faster communication is not automatically faster correct pathfinding.

I am more optimistic about eventually evolving a useful maze algorithm than about beating compiled BFS on this CPU with the current dense interpreter. Success at the first goal would still be a valuable scientific result. A later efficiency claim should count data movement, initialization, completed output, and, where relevant, amortized training cost.

**The next experiment should test the ability to learn a transferable skill under calibrated conditions.** I recommend a calibration-first study centered on switched-goal forks and short complete routes, keeping random-origin discovery central:

1. Establish an independent positive-control panel under the current inputs and budgets: a known working program and graded damaged versions. Check which intermediate states are rewarded, at what times, and whether mutation can repair modest damage. These controls must remain completely outside random-origin populations. The old seeded experiment showed small simplification in the presence of an intact seed; it did not establish repair from meaningful damage.
2. Make developmental observations consistent with achievable propagation times and ordinary lifecycle behavior. Compare several bounded horizons or a preregistered distance-aware schedule. Retain exact completed routes as the primary outcome. Known controls should demonstrate that useful progress is visible before another shaping term is trusted.
3. Use genuinely small, solvable training stages with both goals, varied orientations, reflections, and changing fork geometry; retain earlier competencies during progression. Advance on demonstrated performance, rather than only after a fixed number of generations. V10 moved goals closer on otherwise hard layouts; it did not test a complete progression from reliable elementary tasks. Preserve the native lineages, and include a separately labeled fresh random cohort under the modern stimulus conventions to assess historical specialization.
4. Once those checks pass, allocate one fixed 60–90-minute experiment to a stable primary comparison. Profile first and preregister population, generations, work limits, and replication; target meaningful mutant births and evolutionary depth. Keep local/multirange references and frozen controls, and restore an evaluation-budget-matched resampling control. Avoid spreading the budget across a large collection of simultaneous design changes. Preserve all population/RNG checkpoints and archive discoveries even if active protection is later bounded.

The new final corpus must exclude all 9,296 whole layouts seen through V11, plus any calibration layouts. Reused final-set diagnostics in this audit are hypothesis-generating only. They must not become claims of prospective improvement.

**The decisions should depend on what fails, not just whether a score rises.**

| Result of the next calibrated study | Research implication |
|---|---|
| Known working programs behave incorrectly, or useful partial programs get no usable credit. | Fix runtime conventions, task timing, or the evaluation landscape before scaling search. |
| Lightly damaged programs cannot be repaired, while intact controls work. | Investigate mutation locality and program organization. More population alone is poorly justified. |
| Repair works but random populations cannot acquire even elementary transferable skills. | Focus on bootstrapping, representation, and the transition between skills. |
| Random-origin skills transfer across fresh layouts and orientations, then fail as distance/size increases. | Stronger reason to spend on evolutionary time, population breadth, and developmental scaling. |
| Only proxy scores improve after a precommitted calibrated larger run. | Stop extending this particular register-program/search recipe unchanged; preserve the lineages and test a materially different evolvable organization. |

The milestone I would regard as genuinely encouraging is **replicated growth in completed correct path length and switched-goal success on fresh geometry, surviving rotations and both tree/cyclic families, above frozen and matched resampling controls**. Exact shortestness should be reported separately from mere legality. One more hidden-state difference, isolated training success, or longer genome would not meet that standard.
